Database Tables and Relationships

Previous Course Home Next Lesson
100 minutes Phase 6 · Data Modeling & Database Design Relationships, ER Diagram

Why This Matters

Tables are powerful on their own, but they become truly useful when they connect. In a library, users borrow books. In a shop, customers place orders. These connections are called relationships, and they are the backbone of any database.

What You Will Learn

  • Define relationships between tables.
  • Create an entity-relationship (ER) diagram.
  • Apply primary and foreign keys.

Words to Know

relationship entity ER diagram primary key foreign key one-to-many many-to-many

Lesson Journey (100 minutes)

Part 1 — How Do Tables Connect? 0–10 min

Ask yourself: How do tables connect in a database?

  • One user borrows many books.
  • One book is borrowed many times.

Relationships connect tables.

Part 2 — Relationships and Keys 10–30 min

Learn the key terms:

  • Primary key: unique ID for each record (BookID, UserID).
  • Foreign key: a field that links to another table (UserID in Borrowing table).
  • One-to-many: one user borrows many books.
  • Many-to-many: many users borrow many books (via a link table).

Example ER diagram:

  • Users (UserID, Name, Email).
  • Books (BookID, Title, Author, Genre).
  • Borrowing (BorrowID, UserID, BookID, Date).

Part 3 — Draw an ER Diagram Together 30–55 min

Draw the ER diagram for the library system.

Label primary and foreign keys.

Part 4 — Your Turn: Create Your ER Diagram 55–85 min

Create your ER diagram:

  • At least 3 tables.
  • Primary keys.
  • Foreign keys.
  • Relationships.

Part 5 — Share and Discuss 85–95 min

Present your ER diagram.

Discuss: What is the most complex relationship in your project?

Part 6 — Exit Ticket 95–100 min

Draw a simple ER diagram with 2 tables and a relationship.

Check Your Understanding

You are ready to move on when you can create an ER diagram with relationships.

Support: Use an ER diagram template.

Challenge: Normalize your tables.
