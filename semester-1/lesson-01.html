Welcome, Thinker!

This year, you are not just learning to code. You are becoming a project builder — someone who looks at a real problem in the world and designs a system to solve it.

But before you build, you need a way of thinking. That way of thinking is called computational thinking. Think of it as the roots of a tree: you can't see them, but everything above the ground depends on them.

In this first lesson, you will learn what computational thinking is, how it works at the scale of a full project, and you will choose the domain your year-long project will grow in.

What You Will Learn

  • Define computational thinking and its four pillars at project scale.
  • Tell the difference between a program, a system, and a project.
  • Choose a domain for your year-long project.

Words to Know

Lesson Journey (100 minutes)

Part 1 — Program, System, or Project? 0–10 min

Look at these three things:

  • A calculator app
  • A hospital patient tracking system
  • A smart farm project

What is the difference? Think about it for a moment.

A program does one job. A system connects many programs and data together. A project is a planned effort with real goals, real users, and real impact.

This year, you will build a project — not just a program.

Part 2 — The Four Pillars at Project Scale 10–30 min

Computational thinking rests on four pillars. At Grade 10, you use all four at the scale of a whole project:

  • Decomposition — break the project into sub-systems: data, interface, logic, output.
  • Pattern Recognition — find patterns in user behaviour, data, and similar projects.
  • Abstraction — create models of the system: architecture diagrams and data flow.
  • Algorithm Design — write the logic for each component and how they connect.

At Grade 10, you apply all four pillars to a full project that integrates another subject and connects to real life.

Part 3 — A Sample Project: Automatic Plant Control 30–55 min

Imagine a system that waters a plant automatically. Let's analyse it through the four pillars together:

  • Decomposition: sensor module, decision logic, water pump, user interface.
  • Pattern recognition: plants need water when soil is dry; weather patterns matter.
  • Abstraction: a simple model — soil moisture → threshold → pump action.
  • Algorithm: read sensor → compare to threshold → turn pump on/off → log data.

Notice how the whole system becomes clear once we break it down. That is the power of computational thinking.

Part 4 — Your Turn: Choose Your Domain 55–85 min

In groups of 3–4, choose a domain for your year-long project. Your teacher will give you a domain card. Options include:

  • Health care system
  • Automatic plant control
  • Agricultural transport routing
  • Interactive library system
  • Energy management
  • Food and nutrition tracking
  • Marketing and trade
  • Environment monitoring

Discuss in your group:

  • What problem in this domain matters to you?
  • Who would use your project?
  • What would success look like?

Write your first journal entry together.

Part 5 — Share and Discuss 85–95 min

Share your domain and your first idea with the class.

Think about this question: What makes a project meaningful?

Part 6 — Exit Ticket 95–100 min

In one sentence, explain the difference between a program, a system, and a project.

Check Your Understanding

You are ready to move on when you can:

  • Explain the four pillars at project scale.
  • Identify a domain for your project.

Support: Use a domain selection template with guiding questions.

Challenge: Can you identify two possible projects in your domain?
